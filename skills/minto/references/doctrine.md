# Minto Doctrine — Grounded Reference

> Verified distillation of Barbara Minto's teachings. Research date: 2026-08-28.
> Every claim below is tagged by grounding: **[P-DIRECT]** = Minto's own words from
> sources she controls (barbaraminto.com) or her recorded voice; **[P-BOOK]** = the
> book's text with page citations as reproduced by named third-party hosts
> (secondhand transmission of her words); **[SECONDARY]** = corroboration only —
> no rule below originates from a secondary source. Unverifiable claims were
> dropped; conflicts are reported, not smoothed over. See Sources at the end and
> the Myth Ledger for claims this skill must NEVER repeat.

Editions: **1978** self-published original → **1987** trade edition (*The Pyramid
Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking*, Pitman; reissued FT Prentice Hall
2002/2010) → **1996** renamed, expanded, self-published edition (*The Minto
Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving*, Minto Books

from the 1996-lineage text. Note: the Pearson "3rd edition" in bookstores is the
*older, shorter* text — not the 1996 edition. [P-DIRECT — barbaraminto.com/textbook;

## 1. The pyramid and "answer first"

- Core claim, her own words: thinking is easy to grasp when you "present the
  ideas organized as a pyramid under a single point." [P-DIRECT — barbaraminto.com]
- Her model of thinking: group low-level facts, draw an insight from their
  similarity, group related insights upward until one thought remains — extended
  thinking "eventually ends in a single pyramid of ideas"; communicating "requires
  only that you guide the reader down the pyramid." [P-DIRECT — /concept]
- Why it works: the mind automatically groups what it receives (she invokes
  Miller's "magical number seven, plus or minus two"); the reader will impose a
  structure anyway, and it will rarely be yours — so state the governing structure
  up front. [P-BOOK — ch. 1]
- The clearest sequence: "give the summarizing idea before the individual ideas
  being summarized." Answer first, always. [P-BOOK — ch. 1]
- Vocabulary note: the top box is "a single point" / "a single thought" in her own
  wording; "governing thought" as a label is consulting vocabulary, not verifiably
  hers. [P-DIRECT / SECONDARY]

## 2. The three rules (the test of a correctly built pyramid)

[P-BOOK — 1996 ed. ch. 1]

1. Ideas at any level must be **summaries** of the ideas grouped below them.
2. "Ideas in each grouping must always be the same kind of idea."
3. "Ideas in each grouping must always be logically ordered."

## 3. Vertical logic — the question/answer dialogue

- "Any point you make must raise a question in the reader's mind" (p. 16), which
  the line below must answer; the document is a continuing Q/A dialogue between
  levels. [P-BOOK — ch. 2]
- You may present only information that answers a question already raised — the
  structure forces you to present information only as the reader needs it.
  [P-BOOK — ch. 2]

## 4. Horizontal logic — deduction vs induction

- Points on a line answer the question above **either** inductively (a group of
  like ideas) **or** deductively (a chained argument) — "one or the other, but not
  both at once" (p. 17). [P-BOOK — ch. 2]
- Deduction limits: at most four points in a deductive argument; at most two
  chained "therefore" links (p. 68). [P-BOOK — ch. 5]
- **Minto's stated preference:** at the Key Line (the level under the main point),
  avoid deduction — "strive instead always to present your message inductively."
  A deductive key line forces readers to re-enact your whole problem-solving
  process before reaching what they care about; "inductive reasoning is always
  easier to absorb at higher levels" (p. 67); "it is always better to present the
  action before the argument" (p. 66). Deduction is fine pushed low (paragraph
  level). [P-BOOK — ch. 5]

## 5. The introduction — SCQ

- The introduction is a **story**: it retells what the reader already knows to
  establish agreed ground — it sets forth "the Situation within which a
  Complication developed that triggered the Question" the document answers
  (p. 19). [P-BOOK — ch. 2/4]
- Elements [P-BOOK — ch. 4, p. 37]:
  - **Situation** — an "established truth about the subject"; what the reader
    already knows, told as narrative.
  - **Complication** — "what happened next to trigger the question"; the change
    to a previously stable situation.
  - **Question** — what the complication raises for the reader; defines scope.
  - **Answer** — your main point; the top of the pyramid, supported by the Key Line.
- **Where the Situation starts:** with a self-sufficient AND noncontroversial
  statement — something "you know the reader will agree is true." Beginners:
  put historical chronology in the introduction; limit the introduction to what
  the reader will accept as true. [P-BOOK — ch. 3/4]
- Introduction length tracks the reader's needs, not document length (p. 43).
  [P-BOOK]
- Story-order variants (S, C, A re-sequenced for tone; lower confidence — witness
  labels diverge): standard/"considered" S→C→A; direct A→S→C; concerned C→S→A;
  "aggressive" Q→S→C (single witness). [P-BOOK — ch. 4, thinner attestation]
- Her trademarked framing is **SCQ** (Situation–Complication–Question). SCQA/SCQR
  four-letter labels are third-party derivatives. [P-DIRECT — barbaraminto.com]

## 6. MECE

- The book's wording (concept, not acronym): when dividing a whole into parts,
  the pieces must be "mutually exclusive of each other" and "collectively
  exhaustive in terms of the whole" (p. 82, ch. 6). [P-BOOK]
- Whether the acronym "MECE" appears in the book text is unverified — the acronym
  is verified as Minto/McKinsey vocabulary via her interview. [flagged]
- Origin: Minto invented it during her McKinsey years — her verbatim line titles
  McKinsey's alumni interview: "MECE: I invented it, so I get to say how to
  pronounce it" (she says "meece"). [P-DIRECT — mckinsey.com/alumni, title quote]

## 7. Grouping and summarizing rules

[P-BOOK — 1996 ed. ch. 7]

- **Ban on "intellectually blank" assertions:** statements like "The company
  faces three problems" or headings like "Background" / "Findings" are blank —
  "they simply state the *kind* of idea that will be discussed" (p. 94) instead
  of summarizing the essence below. Every grouping implies a point; state it.
- The two derivation rules (p. 97):
  - "Summarize the action ideas by stating the effect of carrying out the actions."
  - "Summarize the situation ideas by stating what their being similar implies."
- The more specific the summary, the easier the completeness check (p. 118).
- A grouping's common property shows when the sentences share the same kind of
  subject, same kind of predicate, or imply the same kind of judgement (p. 111).

## 8. The three orders for inductive groupings

[P-BOOK — 1996 ed. ch. 6, p. 76, 92–93]

Every inductive grouping must carry an order revealing the analytical activity
that produced it:

- **Time (chronological)** — from determining cause-effect: steps in a process
  always go in time order.
- **Structural** — from commenting on a structure: divide a whole into its parts
  (where the mutually-exclusive / collectively-exhaustive test lives, p. 82).
- **Degree (ranking)** — from classification: classify like things, then rank
  (e.g., by importance).
- Checking discipline (p. 92–93): identify the type of point, group same types,
  look for the implied order; if none of time/structure/degree fits, the grouping
  itself is suspect.
- Edition note: the 1987/2002-lineage text presents **four** orders (deductive +
  the three above) — a presentation difference, not a doctrinal conflict.

## 9. Logic in problem solving (Part 3, 1996 ed.)

- **Sequential Analysis** (p. 121): answer questions in logical sequence — Is
  there a problem? Where does it lie? Why does it exist? What could we do about
  it? What should we do about it? (The book credits B. Robert Holland's
  "Sequential analysis" manual.) [P-BOOK]
- **Defining the problem — R1/R2 gap:** a problem is the gap between **R1, the
  Undesired Result** (status quo) and **R2, the Desired Result** (p. 127). The
  problem-definition story has an Opening Scene, a Disturbing Event, R1, and R2.
  The reader's position determines the Question — e.g., they don't know how to
  get from R1 to R2, or think they know but aren't certain (p. 131). The problem
  definition maps directly onto the introduction: it becomes the document's
  S-C-Q (alternatives already known to the reader "always go in the
  Complication," p. 135). [P-BOOK]
- **Structuring the analysis:** where causes aren't evident, visualize the logical
  structure that must produce the observed results using logic trees —
  financial/ROI trees, task structures, activity structures (causes ordered
  strongest-to-weakest), choice/decision trees of yes-no questions, sequential
  structures. An **issue** is "a question so phrased as to require a yes-or-no
  answer," permitting proof or disproof of a hypothesized cause. [P-BOOK]
- Link to the pyramid: analysis trees generate the findings and conclusions that
  populate the pyramid; findings leading to no conclusion, and conclusions
  resting on no finding, are both banned. [P-BOOK]

## 10. Logic in presentation (Part 4, 1996 ed.)

- **On the page** (ch. 10, p. 171–176): short documents — underline the points so
  they jump out; longer documents — introduce the points, then reflect them with
  headings; the document must read as smooth prose *without* the headings; never
  let a major section heading begin immediately after the title (introduce the
  group first); "headings are meant to show divisions of thought" — they state
  ideas, never blank category labels. [P-BOOK]
- Device list — hierarchical headings, underlined points, decimal numbering,
  indented display, dot-dash outlines. [P-BOOK, thinner attestation]
- **On slides** (ch. 11): one point per text slide, terse (roughly ≤6 lines /
  ~30 words — single witness for the numbers); the deck alternates text slides
  (the pyramid's points) with exhibit slides; the storyboard is drafted from the
  pyramid before any slide is made. [P-BOOK, single-witness numerics flagged]

## 11. The prescribed working process

- **Top-down first** (ch. 3): always try top-down before anything else. Steps:
  (1) draw the top box, note the subject; (2) decide the Question — who you are
  writing to and what they need answered; (3) write the Answer if you know it;
  (4) identify the Situation — the first noncontroversial statement about the
  subject; (5) develop the Complication — what happened to trigger the reader's
  question; (6) recheck that the Complication actually raises the Question your
  Answer answers. [P-BOOK — ch. 3]
- **Bottom-up when the top isn't clear:** list all the points you want to make,
  work out the relationships between them, draw conclusions — build groupings
  upward until a single thought emerges. If thinking is undeveloped, start
  anywhere in the pyramid and let it pull other ideas out. [P-BOOK — ch. 3]
- Beginner caveats (ch. 3, each verified in ≥2 carriers): try top-down first;
  begin thinking with the Situation; think the introduction through on paper,
  not in your head; put historical chronology in the introduction, not the body;
  limit the introduction to points the reader will accept as true. [P-BOOK]
- Thinking runs bottom-up; communication runs top-down — her site describes
  exactly this build-up, then "guide the reader down." The aphorism "think
  bottom-up, present top-down" is interpreters' compression. [P-DIRECT / SECONDARY]

## 12. The question-led process

- The core course skill is "how to identify the reader's question," then using
  the pyramid rules to discover and organize the points that answer it.
  [P-DIRECT — /course]
- The SCQ Framework exists "for identifying the question in your reader's mind" —
  it puts writer and reader in the same place before the thinking is presented.
  [P-DIRECT — barbaraminto.com; P-BOOK fragment]
- Don't raise questions before you're ready to answer them; don't answer
  questions you haven't raised. [P-BOOK — chs. 1–2]
- Note: "you cannot know what to include until you know the question" is a
  paraphrase, not traceable to Minto verbatim. [verification negative]

## 13. The writing stage after structuring

[P-BOOK — chs. 4, 10, 12 unless noted]

- Introductions "remind rather than inform" — a story the reader already knows
  (her illustration: children's favourite stories are "ones they already know");
  nothing the reader would dispute; typically 2–3 paragraphs, can be one sentence
  for a familiar reader.
- Section openings: state the grouping's summarizing idea at the top of the
  section before its support; introduce a group of headings in text before using
  them.
- Transitions: at group boundaries look "in both directions at once" — carry a
  key word or phrase from the previous section into the point of the next; after
  a long or complex section, summarize fully before moving on.
- Endings: concluding paragraphs are discouraged (hard to do well, usually
  unnecessary); a next-steps section limited to immediate actions works better.
- **The 30-second test:** the introduction plus Key Line points should give the
  reader the entire gist in about 30 seconds.
- Prose (ch. 12): good ideas "ought not to be dressed up in bad prose";
  visualize the relationship between ideas as an image, then translate the image
  into sentences.

## 14. Editing existing documents (her own working method)

- Her lifelong method WAS document surgery: editing McKinsey reports, then in her
  courses drawing pictures of participants' document structures and rebuilding
  them. [P-DIRECT — McKinsey interview; /course]
- The diagnostic instrument is the three rules themselves (§2): a document
  failing any test is misstructured. You can't tell nonsense is being written
  until you impose a structure on it. [P-BOOK — chs. 1, 6–7]
- Named reviewer failure-pattern: intellectually blank assertions (§7). The fix
  is completing the thinking until the summary states an action's effect or a
  situation's implication. Also check that "three reasons" really are three, and
  only three. [P-BOOK — ch. 7; P-DIRECT — /course]
- Verified diagnosis quotes: "The problem was the thinking, not the language";
  "The pyramid is a tool to help you find out what you think." The popular
  "clarity of writing reflects clarity of thinking" aphorism is NOT traceable to
  her. [P-DIRECT — McKinsey interview; verification negative]

## 15. Stated limits (hers, vs later interpreters')

- Scope, her words: documents whose "purpose is to offer your thinking to a
  reader" — expository business communication, not writing generally.
  [P-DIRECT — /concept]
- Her narrow answer-first softening (ch. 5): put argument before action if the
  reader "is going to disagree strongly with your conclusion," or cannot
  understand the action without prior explanation. This governs ordering WITHIN
  the pyramid — it is not a licence to abandon it. [P-BOOK]
- She permits reordering S-C-Q for tone (§5 variants). [P-BOOK — ch. 4]
- Slides built her way are deliberately not self-explanatory as handouts —
  transitional/introductory points belong in what is said aloud. [P-BOOK — ch. 11]
- "Don't use the pyramid on hostile audiences / for storytelling / for bad news"
  is later interpreters' advice — no primary source has Minto saying it.
  [SECONDARY — origin confirmed non-Minto]
- MECE attribution humility, her words: "it wasn't really I, actually, it was
  Aristotle" — she claimed the abbreviation and its application to groups of
  ideas. [P-DIRECT — McKinsey interview]

## Biography (verified, compressed)

- Cleveland, Ohio. HBS MBA **Class of 1963** — among the first women to earn the
  HBS MBA, via the Harvard–Radcliffe second-year route. [P — hbs1963.com;
  library.hbs.edu]
- McKinsey 1963–1973: "the first female MBA professional hire the firm ever made"
  (McKinsey's own phrasing). London from 1966 to improve European staff writing;
  laid off 1973 in the oil-embargo downturn and stayed in London.
  [P — mckinsey.com/alumni; hbs1963.com]
- Founded Minto International, Inc. (1973). Taught McKinsey, most major
  consultancies and their clients; lectured at Harvard, MIT, Stanford, Chicago,
  London, Manchester. Her method remains in McKinsey onboarding. [P — /course]
- Materials she sells: the 1996 textbook; a self-study course (DVD + 192-page
  workbook); a 5-part online video course; a course app. Trademarks: "The Minto
  Pyramid Principle®", "SCQ Framework℠". No licensed trainers or successor
  organization are named anywhere. [P-DIRECT — barbaraminto.com]

## Myth Ledger — never repeat these

1. **"Minto died in 2024."** Unverified and apparently false — no obituary
   exists; name-matched obituaries are different people (checked 2026-08-28).
2. **"First female MBA graduate of HBS."** Inflated — one of a small handful of
   women in the Class of 1963; the first *directly admitted* women graduated 1965.
3. **"First woman at McKinsey."** The verified claim is "first female **MBA
   professional hire**."
4. **"MECE was invented by McKinsey."** Minto invented it while at the firm.
5. **"The Pyramid Principle is an official McKinsey framework."** It is Minto's
   personal, trademarked IP, taught *to* McKinsey and others.
6. **"First published 1985."** Library records: 1978 self-published; 1987 first
   trade edition.
7. **"SCQA/SCQR is Minto's label."** Her trademark is SCQ; four-letter variants
   are derivatives (the A — the Answer — is still doctrine; only the label differs).
8. **A specific "1972 McKinsey staff paper."** Plausible, no primary record — do
   not cite one.
